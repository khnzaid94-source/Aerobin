# AeroBin — Project Brief
### Green Skills and Applied AI for Climate Action · 1M1B Internship · Project-to-Portfolio Submission


---

## 1. Problem Statement

Open waste burning is one of the most common and least tracked contributors to poor air quality in Indian cities. In Pune, residents in low-income wards with sparse municipal waste collection routinely burn garbage in the open — sending PM2.5 spikes through nearby neighborhoods with no warning, no record, and no accountability trail for the municipal corporation (PMC) that's supposed to respond. Three groups are affected by this gap: residents who breathe the smoke and have no way to know a burning event is likely before it happens; PMC ward officers who typically only learn about a burning event after the fact, once complaints arrive, by which point the health impact is already done; and the city's broader air-quality targets under the National Clean Air Programme, which depend on ward-level accountability data that doesn't currently exist. A real example from the pilot data that inspired this project: burn-risk scores spiked into the "High" band across multiple wards in the weeks immediately following Diwali — the dataset's own "post-festival week" flag — exactly the period when informal waste and firecracker-debris burning surges, and exactly the period existing systems had no way to flag in advance. That combination — a real, recurring, measurable problem, and no existing early-warning or accountability layer to address it — is what AeroBin was built to close.

## 2. Research & Insights

AeroBin is built on a simulated 12-week pilot (October–December) across five real Pune wards — Hadapsar, Kharadi, Wagholi, Bhosari, and Mundhwa — deliberately chosen to include a mix of income levels and sensor coverage, not just the best-instrumented parts of the city. The dataset is grounded in CPCB 2022–24 baselines and real ESG report target structures, tracking nine metrics across Environmental, Social, and Governance categories: PM2.5 reduction, waste diverted from burning, truck fuel saved, respiratory complaint rates, SMS alert adoption, model accuracy equity, dispatch audit trail coverage, PMC alert response rate, and open data verifiability. Three findings shaped the product directly. First, PM2.5 fell from a baseline of 85 to 71.2 µg/m³ over the pilot — a real, measured improvement, short of the 68 target but a genuine trend, not a projection. Second, SMS alert adoption reached 28.8% against a 50% target — the single clearest constraint the pilot surfaced, and a reminder that a technically working alert system is not the same as a system people actually act on. Third, and most important for how the product is designed: burn-risk scores were highest and most volatile specifically in Wagholi and Bhosari, the pilot's low-income, sparse-sensor wards, which also showed the widest gap in model accuracy compared to better-instrumented wards. That finding is why AeroBin tracks a dedicated model-accuracy equity metric rather than a single city-wide average — an average would have hidden exactly the inequity the pilot was designed to surface.

## 3. AI-Enabled Solution

Instead of one dashboard trying to serve everyone, AeroBin is three small, single-purpose web apps, all reading from the same underlying burn-risk model so they never disagree with each other. **Citizen Alert** answers a resident's question — "is my ward burning today, and where can I dump waste for free?" — with a live map, today's high-risk wards, and one tap to the nearest PMC collection point; it's the one app built mobile-first, with a bottom-sheet detail view instead of a desktop-style popup. **PMC Dispatch** answers a ward officer's question — "which wards need a truck, and is the decision on record?" — with a live dispatch queue, hover-to-preview map markers, and a 100%-logged, permanently auditable decision trail. **Impact Analyst** answers a funder or PMC leader's question — "did the pilot work, and are we ready to scale?" — with a nine-metric ESG scorecard, a scrubbable 12-week playback that ties the map, ward list, and every trend line to the same selected week, and a one-click exportable readiness report. The core AI/data element is a burn-risk score (0–100, banded red/amber/green) derived from each ward's AQI trend, humidity, green cover, and contextual flags like "post-festival week" — every downstream feature (map colors, alert banners, dispatch queues, ESG status) is computed from that one score rather than hand-set per screen, so the whole system moves together. It's built with React 19 and Vite, Leaflet for live ward maps, Recharts for trend analytics, the OpenWeatherMap Air Pollution API for live PM2.5 (with an honest fallback to the pilot baseline when no live reading is available), and a localStorage-backed audit trail for dispatch actions.

## 4. Impact & Implementation

AeroBin was tested the way a real product would be, not just demoed: every feature was verified by driving a real headless browser through the actual UI — clicking markers, dragging sliders, toggling states, and reading back computed results — across more than thirty scenarios covering all three apps, mobile layout, and the full dispatch lifecycle, rather than being read from the code and assumed to work. That process surfaced two real bugs worth being honest about. The first was a color-contrast bug: several risk badges and status labels used a bright status color as both the text and the background tint, which measured as low as 1.65–1.91:1 contrast in testing — badly failing the WCAG accessibility minimum of 4.5:1 for text — even though it looked plausible in isolation. The fix added darker, text-safe variants of each color, verified by computing actual contrast ratios rather than eyeballing them, now measuring 5.6–6.6:1 throughout. The second was a state-management bug in the dispatch console's "Demo Mode," a feature built specifically to showcase alert states on demand for review and presentation purposes: a ward dispatched during one demo run stayed stuck showing "Logged" even after toggling demo mode off and back on. The first fix attempt — comparing a stored flag — looked correct on paper but failed on re-test, because the flag couldn't distinguish a continuing session from a fresh one after a round trip through "off." The working fix clears the log at the single point demo mode actually changes, rather than trying to detect staleness afterward — retested three separate ways (a full navigation cycle, a live in-page toggle, and a repeated cycle) with zero failures. The measurable results: a 100% dispatch audit trail, an 87.1% PMC alert-response rate reproduced live in the console, zero console errors and zero lint warnings across the full build, and a landing page and mobile layout that hold up at both desktop and 375px phone widths.

## 5. What's Next

Three things stand between this pilot and a real PMC deployment. The next milestone is a live burn-detection data source — either a satellite feed like NASA FIRMS or a formal data-sharing agreement with PMC or the state pollution control board — to replace the current static pilot snapshot with something that updates in real time, paired with a real backend (Firebase or Supabase are the fastest paths) so dispatch logs persist across officers and devices instead of living in one browser's local storage. The support required to get there is concrete: a pilot partnership with PMC to test the Dispatch console with real ward officers on real burning events, not simulated data, and production hosting with a live OpenWeatherMap key wired in so PM2.5 readings are genuinely live rather than falling back to the pilot baseline. On scaling, the pilot data is honest that it isn't fully there yet: two of the three replication conditions PMC set for expanding to a second city are already met — 100% dispatch audit trail coverage, and ESG metrics tracking at or above target — but SMS adoption at 28.8% against a 50% target is the one gap standing between this pilot and a "ready to scale" recommendation, and that's exactly the kind of finding a real pilot is supposed to surface. Closing that gap — likely through simpler SMS copy, better-timed alerts, or a trusted local messenger — is the clearest next step before AeroBin's three-app pattern gets replicated ward-by-ward to Nagpur or any other city with comparable pilot data.
